The 15-minute sales call (and "I can't afford it")

A good sales call is mostly listening. Ask a few questions, show them their own business, recommend one option, and ask for a decision. No pressure, no promises you can't keep.

Before the call (5 minutes)

  • Their numbers: Google rating, review count, who shows up above them for "[service] [town]".
  • The preview site open on your phone or laptop.
  • Your three packages and prices, and the one you'll recommend.

The call

1. Open (30 seconds): "Thanks for making time. I'll ask a few quick questions, show you the preview, and you tell me if it's useful. Sound good?"

2. Ask (5 minutes), then listen:

  1. "How do most new customers find you right now?"
  2. "Roughly how many calls or inquiries do you get in a normal week?"
  3. "What's an average job worth to you?"
  4. "Out of 10 people who call, how many end up booking?"
  5. "What kind of jobs do you want more of?"

Write their answers in their words. You'll use them in the proposal.

3. Show (5 minutes): walk through the preview using what they just told you: "You said you want more [deck jobs] in [town], so here's the page for exactly that."

4. Recommend one option (2 minutes): "Based on what you said, I'd go with [Get Calls]: [two lines of what it does]. It's [price]."

5. Ask for a decision: "Want me to get started? It's 50% today and you're live in [X] days." Then stop talking.

The money math (use their numbers, not yours)

One extra job a month is worth: job value × how many callers book. Example: one job = $450, half of callers book, you charge $300/month:

  • 2 extra calls a month → about 1 extra job → $450 a month for the business
  • your monthly fee is covered by about 0.7 extra jobs a month

Say it like this: "One job is worth about $450 to you. If the site brings even one extra job a month, it pays for itself. I can't promise how many calls you'll get, nobody honest can, but that's the bar."

"I can't afford it" (and 7 other things you'll hear)

"It's too expensive." / "I can't afford it." Usually it means "I'm not sure it'll pay off", not "I have no money". Don't drop your price. Ask: "Is it the total, or not being sure it'll pay off?" Then either show the money math, or offer the smaller package or a payment plan for the setup.

"Send me something and I'll think about it." "Happy to. Which part do you want to think about?" Answer it, send the proposal today, and book a time: "I'll call you Friday at 10."

"We get enough work from word of mouth." "That's the best kind of business. Most word of mouth ends with someone Googling your name. Right now that lands on [Yelp/nothing]."

"My nephew does websites." "Great, keep the preview as ideas for him. If it ever doesn't work out, call me."

"I tried a website before and it did nothing." "What happened with it?" Listen. "Most sites fail because nobody updates them and Google never sees them. This one has a page for every service and town, and you'll see what changes every month."

"Can you guarantee page one?" "No, and be careful with anyone who does. Google decides rankings. I guarantee the work, and you'll see it every month."

"I don't want a monthly fee." "No problem, the one-time package gets you live and it's yours. You can add the monthly part later."

"Can I see other sites you've built?" "You're looking at the best example: I built this one for you."

After the call

  • Proposal within 2 hours, in their words, with the package they picked.
  • Payment terms: 50% to start, 50% at launch. Monthly billed in advance, 30 days' notice to cancel.
  • A simple written agreement: what's included, the timeline, who owns the domain and content (they do), how to cancel.
  • If they said "think about it": the follow-up call you booked. If they said no: thank them, and leave the door open.
